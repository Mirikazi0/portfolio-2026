What this project was and wasn't

Argued through data before Figma.

My PM brought me a brief: the pricing page wasn't converting the way it should, and the sign-up flow felt cold. At a product like Interview Query, which runs real A/B tests and tracks revenue down to the billing interval, redesigning these flows requires evidence, not gut feeling.

Before opening Figma, I gathered evidence. I ran competitor teardowns on DataLemur, Exponent, and LeetCode, interviewed candidates about their hesitation points, and aligned with the PM on experiment architecture.

Most of the shaping work, including competitor breakdowns, user intent mapping, and tier hierarchy logic, happened before any visual file existed. Figma came in once we agreed on the architecture.

The problem

Two plans. Zero clarity.

Two plans.
Zero clarity.

Interview Query had two subscription tiers: IQ Coder ($29/mo) and IQ Premium ($79/mo). Traffic was coming. But visitors couldn't tell the difference between the plans fast enough to act.

Key features were buried. High-value Yearly plans were hidden behind tabs. Coaching was split into a separate card competing with main subscriptions. The sign-up flow showed the exact same generic screen to a senior FAANG candidate as an entry-level analyst.

In interviews, candidates kept asking: "Do I need Premium or is Coder enough for FAANG prep?" Tier names gave no answer, and long feature lists forced users to guess.

Research synthesis

Four design implications.

1.
People buy for their goal, not a feature list.

Reframe each tier around what it gets you, such as FAANG prep, company-specific guides, and mock interviews, rather than a generic checklist of inclusions.

2.
Default to the plan that wins.

Default to the annual view. Setting the primary anchor price to $17/mo instead of $79/mo increased annual selections.

3.
Add-ons should extend a decision, not force a new one.

Coaching as a radio selector inside Premium means users decide once, not twice. One checkout flow, not two competing products.

4.
Show candidates what they'll hit before they hit it.

Amber "Limited" badges on Coder features signal the ceiling without punishing the tier. It creates a natural reason to upgrade rather than pressure.

One framework.
One decision.

Users reached the pricing page with intent, but bounced because the page couldn't answer the primary question: "Which plan helps me pass my interview?"

What would have proved me wrong

If checkout conversion went up but revenue per user went down, it would have meant users were downgrading to the cheap Coder plan because of savings anchoring. Instead, revenue per user grew by +12%, confirming users understood the value of Premium and annual commitment, not just the lower price.
